Jack and the Beanstalk

Easy English (story prose)

JACK AND THE BEANSTALK
Easy English, written to be read aloud
A new telling from the old public-domain tale

Jack lived with his mother in a little house at the edge of town. They were poor, and they had a cow called Milky-White. Every morning she gave them milk, and every morning Jack took that milk to market and sold it. That was how they bought their bread.

Then one day Milky-White gave no milk at all. Jack’s mother sat at the table with her head in her hands. “What shall we do now?” she said.

“I will sell the cow,” said Jack. “I will get a good price for her, and we will be all right.”

So Jack put a rope on Milky-White and walked her down the road. He had not gone far when he met an old man with bright eyes and a little bag in his hand.

“Good morning, Jack,” said the old man.

Jack stopped. “Good morning. How do you know my name?”

“I know many things,” said the old man. “I know you are going to sell your cow. But I have something better than money.” He opened the bag. Inside were beans, red and green and gold, shining like little lights. “These are magic beans. Plant them tonight, and by morning they will grow all the way up to the sky. Give me the cow, and they are yours.”

Jack looked at the beans, and then he looked at Milky-White. He wanted to do well for his mother. He also wanted to see something wonderful. “Yes,” he said. He gave the man the rope, put the beans in his pocket, and ran home.

“You are back so soon,” said his mother. “Did you sell the cow? How much did you get?”

“I got these,” said Jack, and he held out the beans. “They are magic. If we plant them tonight, they will grow up to the sky.”

His mother stared at him. “Beans? Jack, we needed money. We needed food. And you brought home beans.” She was not cruel, but she was tired and afraid. She threw the beans out of the window into the dark garden. “Go to bed,” she said softly. “There is no supper tonight.”

Jack climbed the stairs to his little room. He felt sorry, and he felt foolish, and after a while he fell asleep.

When he woke, the room looked strange. Sunlight cut across the floor, but one side of the window was green and dark. Jack got up and looked out.

A beanstalk stood in the garden. It was thicker than a tree, and it went up and up and up, through the clouds, into the sky.

Jack opened the window. The beanstalk was so close that he could step onto it, and it felt like a great green ladder. He began to climb. He climbed through the leaves and the wind and the morning, higher than the birds, higher than the clouds, until at last he stepped off onto a long white road.

He walked along that road until he came to a house so big it made him feel as small as a mouse. A tall woman stood in the doorway.

“Please,” said Jack. “I am very hungry. Could I have a little food?”

The woman looked down the road, then down at Jack. “You should go,” she said. “My husband is a giant, and he does not like boys. He does not like them at all.”

“I have had nothing to eat,” said Jack. “Please.”

The woman sighed, because she had a kind heart. She took him into the kitchen and gave him bread and a cup of milk. Jack had only taken two bites when the floor began to shake.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

“Hide,” whispered the woman, and she helped him into a great pot by the fire. The lid came down just as the door flew open.

The giant filled the room. He sniffed the air, and his voice rolled like thunder.

“Fee-fi-fo-fum!
I smell a boy.
I smell a little one!”

“No, no,” said his wife. “You smell your breakfast. Sit down and eat.”

The giant sat, and he ate, and the table jumped under his hands. When he had finished, he pulled a bag of gold from a chest and began to count it, coin by coin. After a time his head nodded. His eyes closed. He snored, and the windows rattled in their frames.

Jack lifted the lid and climbed out, as quiet as a cat. He took the bag of gold, slipped from the house, and ran. He did not stop until he reached the beanstalk. Then he climbed down, down, down, back through the clouds, and dropped into the garden with the gold in his arms.

“Mother!” he cried. “The beans were magic after all.”

They bought food that day, and wood for the fire, and for a while the little house felt warm again. But gold does not last forever, and one morning the bag was empty.

So Jack climbed the beanstalk once more.

The tall woman was at the door again, and she almost sent him away. Jack talked softly and smiled, and at last she let him in. He hid in the pot. The floor shook. The giant came home.

“Fee-fi-fo-fum! I smell a boy!”

“Eat your breakfast,” said his wife, and the giant ate. Then he called, “Bring me my hen.”

She brought a brown hen and set it on the table. “Lay,” said the giant, and the hen laid an egg of solid gold.

The giant smiled a slow, sleepy smile. Soon he was snoring again.

Jack took the hen and ran. The hen cried, “Cluck! Cluck!” and the giant woke with a roar. “My hen! My hen!” But Jack was already on the road, and then on the beanstalk, climbing down so fast the leaves flew past his face.

Now Jack and his mother had a hen that laid a golden egg whenever Jack said “Lay.” They were not hungry any more. Still, Jack could not stop thinking of the road in the sky. He wanted to see it one last time.

He climbed the beanstalk a third time. This time he did not go to the door. He waited until the woman went to the well, then crept inside and hid in a wooden box. The giant came home, sniffing.

“Fee-fi-fo-fum! I smell a boy, and I know I do!”

He looked in the pot. Jack was not there. He looked in the cupboards. Jack was not there. At last his wife said, “You are dreaming again. Eat.”

After breakfast the giant called, “Bring me my harp.” The harp was gold, and when he said “Sing,” it sang a song so sweet that even Jack, in his box, felt his eyes grow heavy. The giant’s head sank onto his chest. He slept.

Jack opened the box and took the harp. But the harp loved its master, and it cried out, “Help! Help!”

The giant’s eyes flew open. “Stop, boy!”

Jack ran. The giant ran after him. The road seemed longer than before, and the giant’s steps ate up the ground. Jack reached the beanstalk just in time and swung himself down. The whole stalk shook as the giant came after him, down through the clouds, down toward the little house.

“Mother!” Jack shouted. “Bring the axe!”

She came running, the axe in both hands. Jack jumped to the ground, took it from her, and struck the beanstalk with all his strength.

Chop.

The stalk trembled. The giant roared.

Chop.

The beanstalk split and fell, and the giant fell with it, down, down, down, into the deep green hills. He did not get up. He did not come back.

Jack stood in the garden with his mother. He had the golden harp. He had the hen that laid gold eggs. Most of all, he had home.

They were not poor any more. They were safe, and they were together, and they were happy.

The End
